
  His duty was simple. Wind the clockwork. Trim the wick. Polish the lens until he could see his own face in it. Write the log in a
  careful hand: Wind NW, moderate. Visibility good. Lamp lit at 19:42.

  Most entries were dull, and he was glad of it. Dull meant the ships were safe.

  In the autumn of his thirty-second year, a letter came on the Tuesday boat. The mainland office was automating the station. A
  sensor array and a radio beacon would replace the keeper within the season. His service, they wrote, was deeply appreciated.

  Elias read it twice on the rocks, then folded it into his coat and climbed the stairs as usual.

  That night the fog came in thick and fast. He heard the distress horn before he saw anything, a thin, rising cry from the south. A
  fishing trawler, the Marguerite, had lost her bearings in the murk and was drifting toward the reef.

  He did not hesitate. He pushed the lamp to full, cranked the fog signal by hand when the generator faltered, and kept the beam
  sweeping through the grey until, hours later, a dim shape turned away from the rocks and limped toward open water.

  At dawn the sea lay flat and pale. The Marguerite was a speck heading for harbor.

  Elias returned to the lamp room, wiped down the glass, and wrote in the log: Wind calm. Visibility restored. One vessel guided
  clear.

  The automated beacon arrived that winter. It blinked steadily and never tired.

  But when Elias rowed back to the mainland for the last time, he looked over his shoulder and saw the old light still turning, its
  beam sweeping the dark water as it always had, and he understood that some things were worth keeping, even after they were no
  longer needed.
